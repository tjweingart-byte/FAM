what caused the 2008 financial crisis

On the fifteenth of September 2008, Lehman Brothers - a hundred-and-fifty-eight-year-old Wall Street bank - filed for bankruptcy, the largest in American history, and within days the whole financial system was close to seizing up. What brought it there was an ordinary thing: American home loans, millions of them, made to people who were never going to be able to pay them back.

It starts with house prices. Through the early two-thousands they rose year after year, and a rising market makes almost any loan look safe. If a borrower can't pay, the house can be sold for more than is owed. So lenders loosened their standards. They offered subprime mortgages to borrowers with weak credit, loans with low teaser rates that jumped after two years, and loans where nobody checked the borrower's income at all.

The lenders could do that because they didn't keep the loans. Here is the machinery. A mortgage company would sell its loans to a Wall Street bank. The bank would pool thousands of them together and turn the pool into bonds - mortgage-backed securities - that paid investors out of homeowners' monthly payments. Then it sliced those bonds again, into layers. The top layer was paid first and was supposed to be almost riskless; the bottom layer took the first losses and paid the highest return. Some of the riskier layers were pooled and sliced yet again, into what were called collateralised debt obligations.

The credit rating agencies stamped much of this AAA, the same grade as the safest government debt. Their models assumed that homeowners in different parts of the country wouldn't all default at once, because house prices had never fallen nationwide at the same time. Pension funds, insurers and banks across the world bought it, because it paid more than government bonds and was rated as if it were just as safe.

And the banks borrowed heavily to hold it. The big investment banks were running on enormous leverage - in some cases more than thirty dollars of borrowed money for every dollar of their own - much of it borrowed overnight and rolled over every day. That meant a small fall in the value of what they owned could wipe out everything they had, and that their lenders could vanish between one morning and the next.

Then house prices stopped rising. By 2006 they had peaked; by 2007 they were falling across the country, and the teaser rates were resetting. Borrowers who had counted on refinancing or selling couldn't do either. Defaults climbed, and they climbed everywhere at once - the one thing the models said wouldn't happen. The layers that were supposed to be safe started taking losses.

The worst of it was that nobody could tell who held what. These securities were tangled through the whole system, so when the losses came, every bank suspected every other bank of being the one about to fail. Lending between them dried up. In March 2008 Bear Stearns ran out of cash and was sold to JPMorgan in a rescue arranged by the Federal Reserve. In early September the government took over Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the two giants behind much of the country's mortgage market.

Then came the weekend Lehman went to the government looking for the same kind of rescue, and was refused. Officials believed the markets had had time to prepare for it. They hadn't. Lehman's collapse froze lending almost overnight. The next day the government lent eighty-five billion dollars to the insurer AIG, which had sold protection against exactly these bonds defaulting and couldn't pay out. A money market fund that held Lehman's debt fell below a dollar a share - something that was never supposed to happen to what people treated as cash.

In October Congress passed a seven-hundred-billion-dollar rescue fund, and the government put money directly into the largest banks. The Fed cut interest rates to almost zero and began buying bonds on a scale it never had before. The system held. The economy didn't: the recession that followed was the deepest since the nineteen-thirties, and unemployment reached ten percent in the autumn of 2009.

So the cause was less one bad decision than a chain in which every link looked safe from where it stood. The borrower trusted rising prices, the lender trusted that it could sell the loan, the bank trusted the rating, the rating agency trusted the history - and the whole structure stood on the one assumption all of them shared, that American house prices would never fall everywhere at once.
