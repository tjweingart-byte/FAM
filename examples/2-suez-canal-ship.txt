what happened with the ship stuck in the suez canal

In March of twenty twenty-one a container ship a quarter of a mile long sat wedged diagonally across the Suez Canal, its bow buried in the eastern bank and its stern pinned against the western one, and nothing behind it could move.

The Ever Given went in on the twenty-third of March, in a dust storm, with gusts near forty knots pushing on a wall of stacked containers that acted like a sail. In open water that wind is a nuisance. In a channel narrower than the ship is long, it was enough to swing her sideways, and the southern stretch of the canal is a single lane. One ship across it closes the whole thing.

That is why a grounding became a world story. About an eighth of the world's trade goes through Suez, because the only other way between Asia and Europe is round the bottom of Africa, a week or more longer. Lloyd's List, the shipping industry's paper, put the cargo held up at close to ten billion dollars a day. Within days more than three hundred and fifty ships were waiting at either end, carrying everything from oil to livestock.

Pulling her off was slower than anyone hoped, because the problem was never the ship. It was the sand. Tugs could not shift two hundred thousand tonnes sitting on a bank, so dredgers dug around the bow, some thirty thousand cubic metres of it, while the engineers waited on the moon. A spring tide on the twenty-ninth lifted the water just high enough, and she floated free after six days.

The queue cleared within a week. The ship took far longer. Egypt held the Ever Given in a lake halfway along the canal and asked her owners for nearly a billion dollars, and she sat there, loaded, until a settlement in July let her sail on to Rotterdam, nearly four months late.
