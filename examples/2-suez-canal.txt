why does the suez canal matter so much

The Suez Canal is a hundred and twenty miles of water across the Egyptian desert, and it saves a ship sailing between Asia and Europe the trip around the entire continent of Africa.

Without it, a container ship from Shanghai to Rotterdam goes south past the Cape of Good Hope, adding thousands of miles and well over a week at sea. With it, the ship cuts through the Red Sea and straight into the Mediterranean. That shortcut carries roughly twelve percent of world trade: oil and gas heading west, and the containers of electronics, clothes and car parts that fill European shops.

Because it is a single channel, it is also a single point of failure. In March 2021 a giant container ship, the Ever Given, was blown sideways in a sandstorm and wedged itself across the canal. For six days nothing moved. Hundreds of ships queued at either end, some gave up and turned for Africa, and the delays rippled through supply chains for months afterwards.

It has always been worth fighting over, too. The canal opened in 1869, built by a French company and later controlled largely by Britain. In 1956 Egypt's president, Gamal Abdel Nasser, nationalised it, and Britain, France and Israel invaded to take it back - until pressure from the United States and the Soviet Union forced them out. That crisis is remembered as the moment Britain stopped being the power that decided things on its own.

Egypt has run the canal ever since, and the fees ships pay to use it are one of the country's biggest sources of foreign money - which is why a stranded ship, or a war on the Red Sea route, is felt in Cairo as much as in the shops at the other end.
