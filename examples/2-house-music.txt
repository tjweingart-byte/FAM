where did house music originate in america

A bar on the South Side of Chicago, around 1981. Frankie Knuckles is stopped at a light with a friend, sees a sign in the window that says "we play house music", and asks what that is. His friend tells him: it's the music you play at your club. Knuckles didn't know his own sound had a name.

The club was the Warehouse, on South Jefferson Street in the West Loop, and Knuckles had been its resident DJ since 1977, when a promoter named Robert Williams brought him in from New York to recreate the Manhattan discotheque scene in Chicago. The crowd was overwhelmingly Black and gay - people shut out of a lot of the city's other clubs - so this became their own room.

Disco had collapsed commercially around 1980, and the labels had stopped releasing new records. Knuckles had a dancefloor to feed and nothing new to feed it, so he dug into old soul, funk, gospel and jazz, re-editing tracks on reel-to-reel tape, stretching the breaks and layering them into something that had never existed as a single record. When drum machines like Roland's got cheap in the early eighties, he put that relentless four-on-the-floor pulse underneath. Disco's wreckage, gospel's voice and a machine's heartbeat - that combination is what people outside the club started calling house, after the Warehouse itself.

In 1984, Jesse Saunders and Vince Lawrence pressed what's widely considered the first house record, "On and On". Two years later Farley "Jackmaster" Funk's "Love Can't Turn Around" went into the British top ten, and that was the pivot: a sound built in after-hours clubs by Black, gay Chicagoans, with almost no commercial backing at home, was picked up by Europe first and sold back to America as something new.
