what is happening with oil prices

BEFORE:
Brent crude was at ninety-five dollars and forty-five cents a barrel at ten fifty-nine GMT today, just before six on Monday morning Eastern, and it was sliding, down about forty cents on the day. The US benchmark, WTI, was at eighty-eight dollars and forty-two cents, also lower. That makes a second weekly fall in a row. Brent dropped about sixteen percent in the first week of November, its biggest weekly fall since the start of the Covid pandemic, then lost roughly another two and a half percent last week, settling Friday at around ninety-five dollars eighty-four cents. What is pulling it down is a tug between two stories. The first is the conflict with Iran. Early in the month President Trump indicated he was unlikely to order fresh strikes while talks to end the fighting continued, and oil fell hard on the hope of a deal. The shipping lane through the Strait of Hormuz is still the nervous spot. Attacks on tankers nearby keep traders on edge, and on Thursday Iranian claims about tankers on fire pushed Brent back up more than three percent in a single session. The second story is supply, and it is the heavier one. JPMorgan estimated the global market had a surplus of one point one million barrels a day in September, and expected that to widen to one point four million in October. OPEC's own output rose almost two million barrels a day in October, to twenty-seven point sixteen million. When more oil is available than buyers need, prices have nowhere to go but down. This morning's reports describe investors weighing renewed hostilities in the Middle East against that strong regional supply, and the supply side is winning for now.

AFTER:
Oil prices have fallen two weeks running, and on Monday morning they were slipping again. Brent crude, the international benchmark, was at ninety-five dollars and forty-five cents a barrel just before eleven GMT, down about forty cents on the day.

Most of the fall came in a single week. Brent lost sixteen percent in the first week of November, its biggest weekly drop since the start of the Covid pandemic. Last week it gave up about another two and a half, and settled on Friday at ninety-five eighty-four. Even after all that, it is roughly sixty percent higher than it was at the start of the year.

Iran is behind both the fall and the nerves. Last Monday President Trump indicated he was unlikely to order fresh strikes while talks to end the conflict continued, and oil fell again on the hope of a deal. But there is no deal yet, and ships near the Strait of Hormuz are still being attacked. On Thursday, fresh Iranian claims about four tankers pushed Brent up more than three percent in one session.

What stops a jump like that from lasting is supply. JPMorgan estimated that in September the world was producing one point one million barrels a day more than it needed, and expected that gap to widen in October. In October, OPEC alone added almost two million barrels a day.
