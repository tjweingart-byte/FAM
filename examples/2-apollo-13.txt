how did apollo 13 get home

In April 1970, fifty-five hours into the flight and about two hundred thousand miles from Earth, an oxygen tank on Apollo 13 blew apart, and the three men aboard went from heading for the Moon to living in a vehicle that was never built to bring anyone home.

The tank fed the command module, Odyssey - the only part of the spacecraft with a heat shield, the only part that could survive coming back through the atmosphere. Within hours it was running out of power and oxygen. So Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise shut it down to save what was left for the last hour of the trip, and climbed into the lunar module, Aquarius, which was still attached to the nose.

Aquarius was a lander. It was designed to keep two people alive for about two days on the surface of the Moon. It now had to keep three alive for four, and it ran out of something almost at once - not oxygen, but the ability to clean the air. Three people breathing in a space meant for two filled it with carbon dioxide, and the lander's filters were nearly spent. The command module had plenty of spare filters, but they were square, and the lander's slots were round.

Engineers in Houston built a fix from what was on board - plastic bags, cardboard from the flight manuals, hoses from the spacesuits, and duct tape - and read the instructions up over the radio, step by step. The crew built it, and the carbon dioxide started to fall.

The way home was the Moon itself. Rather than turn around, they swung behind it and let its gravity throw them back toward Earth, firing the lander's engine to speed the trip up and to aim. They rode most of the way in the dark, near freezing, to save power.

On the last morning they powered Odyssey back up from its batteries, cut Aquarius loose, and came down in the Pacific on the seventeenth of April - in the one part of the ship that could, carried there by the part that was never meant to.
