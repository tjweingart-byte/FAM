what does it mean when the fed raises rates

When the Federal Reserve raises rates, it is raising one number - the rate banks charge each other to borrow money overnight - and that number quietly sets the price of almost every loan in the country.

The Fed doesn't lend to you. It moves what's called the federal funds rate, and banks build their own prices on top of it. So when the Fed lifts it by a quarter of a point, credit card rates follow within weeks, car loans and business loans get dearer, and mortgage rates, which track longer-term borrowing costs, tend to climb with them.

That is the whole point, and it works through a slightly uncomfortable mechanism. The Fed raises rates when prices are rising too fast. Borrowing becomes more expensive, so people buy fewer houses and cars on credit, companies put off building the new warehouse, and spending across the economy cools. Less money chasing the same goods takes the pressure off prices. The cost is that a slower economy means slower hiring, and sometimes job losses.

It also pays savers. The same rise that makes a loan dearer makes a savings account, a money market fund or a Treasury bond pay more, which is another reason people spend less and keep more.

And it is slow. A rate change takes months, often more than a year, to work its way through to prices and jobs, which is why the Fed moves in small steps and watches. It is steering something heavy, with a long delay between turning the wheel and feeling the car move - and every rate decision is a bet about where the road will be by then.
