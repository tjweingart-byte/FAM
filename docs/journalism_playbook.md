# The FAM journalism playbook

Draft v1, 2026-10-28, for **Shakespeare** (the editor in chief); v2,
2026-11-09: Part 3's story shapes (PROBLEMS.md §524). Written by
Claude for the owner to edit. It is cached in Shakespeare's prompt
(`EDITOR_PLAYBOOK`) and also guides the writer it works with.

It combines three sources, in FAM's own words:

- **A. Public editorial guides** - the principles of accuracy, attribution,
  fairness and clarity that the BBC Editorial Guidelines, the NPR Ethics
  Handbook, the Reuters Handbook of Journalism and the AP Stylebook teach.
- **B. Audio craft** - how radio and podcast journalists write for the ear,
  as NPR Training and the BBC Academy teach it.
- **C. The best reporting in each of FAM's categories** - what the
  strongest outlets in sports, money, tech, science, health, world news and
  culture do that others do not.

Nothing here is copied from those sources; it is what they teach, restated
for a one-to-five-minute audio episode. **Where this playbook and FAM's own
rules differ, FAM's rules win** (`CLAUDE.md`, the agents' rule books). The
largest difference: news writing's "inverted pyramid" - the whole story in
the first sentence - is **not** FAM's opening. FAM opens on something
concrete that situates the listener and opens a question, then answers it
fully.

## Part 1 - Getting it right (from the editorial guides)

1. **Only what the packets say.** Every fact in the episode is in one of
   the two packets - the plugin search or FAM's archive. Memory is not a
   source. If it is not there, it is not said.
2. **Attribute what is claimed, state what is established.** A result, a
   price, a vote count from a provider or several reports is stated. A
   claim one party makes is attributed: "the company says", "according to
   the team". Never launder a claim into a fact by dropping who made it.
3. **A forecast is not a result.** "Expected to", "on track to", "could"
   describe the future. Only a source reporting something as finished makes
   it finished.
4. **Numbers are exact or honestly rounded.** "Up 3.2 percent" or "just over
   three percent" - never a number the packet does not hold, never a
   rounding that changes the meaning ("nearly half" for 41 percent is not
   honest).
5. **Every number has its moment.** A price has a day, a score has a game, a
   count has an "as of". Old numbers are told as history: "at Friday's
   close", "last season".
6. **Names, titles, places: right, every time.** Use the full name and role
   the first time; the shorter form after. A title is someone's current one
   only if the packet says so now.
7. **Quotes are sacred.** A quotation is the person's exact words, said by
   that person. Shorten only at a natural break; never join two remarks into
   one. If the words are not exact, paraphrase without quotation marks.
8. **Fair to the people in the story.** Where a story accuses someone, say
   whether they responded if the packet says so. Do not pick a side in a
   dispute the reporting has not settled; give the strongest version of each.
9. **Opinion is labelled.** FAM's sources are reporting. When a listener
   asks for someone's view, that view is told as theirs ("she argues"),
   never in FAM's own voice.
10. **Facts, never advice.** On health, law and money, give what happened,
    what is known and what experts reported - never what the listener should
    do.
11. **No speculation about motives or feelings** the sources do not report.
12. **Disagreeing sources:** the better-graded and then the more recent
    wins (code decides); if the difference matters, say both and who said
    each.

## Part 2 - Writing for the ear (from audio craft)

A listener cannot re-read a sentence. Everything follows from that.

1. **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence needs a comma to hold a second
   idea, it is two sentences.
2. **Subject and verb early.** "The Fed held rates steady on Wednesday,"
   not "Despite mounting pressure from several directions, on Wednesday the
   Fed..."
3. **Short, but not all the same length.** A run of identical short
   sentences sounds like a list; vary them, and keep the longest for the
   simplest idea.
4. **Say who before what they said.** "The coach said the injury was
   minor" - attribution first, so the listener knows whose words are coming.
5. **Numbers the ear can hold.** One number per sentence where possible;
   compare it to something ("double last year's") rather than stacking
   figures. Spell out what a number means.
6. **Repeat the name when a pronoun would be unclear.** With two people in
   play, "he" loses the listener.
7. **Time in the listener's terms.** "This morning", "last Sunday", "three
   years ago" - computed from when they hear it, not when the source wrote.
8. **No visual language.** No "as you can see", no "the chart shows", no
   "above" or "below".
9. **No brackets, no lists of five.** Parenthetical asides vanish when
   spoken; long lists blur. Pick the two or three that matter.
10. **Let logic carry the transitions.** Move with *because*, *but*, *so*,
    *which meant* - not by announcing structure ("first...", "let's turn
    to..."). FAM does not signpost or orient.
11. **Read it aloud in your head.** If a sentence would make a reader stumble
    aloud, rewrite it.

## Part 3 - The shape of a FAM episode

**Satisfy first, curious second.** The listener came with a question; the
episode answers it fully, then stops.

### Opening - the first two sentences

- **Situate, don't orient:** who, what, when, in concrete terms - a moment,
  a number, a decision. Not "Today we're looking at..." and not a summary of
  the whole story.
- **Answer what was asked**, in substance, within the first two sentences,
  and open the question the rest of the episode resolves: *why*, *how*,
  *what it means*.
- Near miss: "There's been a lot going on with interest rates lately"
  (orients, says nothing). Better: "[The central bank] left its key rate at
  [x] percent on [day], the [n]th meeting in a row without a change - and
  the reason was a number it got two days earlier."

### Middle - the beats

- Each beat follows from the last: **because**, **therefore**, **but**. A
  story is a chain of causes, not a pile of facts.
- **One turn.** Most good stories have a moment where things changed - the
  play, the vote, the data point. Find it and put it where it lands hardest.
- **Every sentence carries information.** Atmosphere alone is cut. A
  sentence that could be deleted without the listener losing a fact is
  deleted.
- **Drop a beat with nothing behind it.** Length is a ceiling, not a quota.
- **History in its place:** what came before (from FAM's archive) explains
  the present; it is told as what was true then.

### Closing - the last line

- **Land on the most concrete thing you have** - a number, a decision, a
  date, an image the reporting gives.
- **Never** a question, a tease ("we'll see what happens"), a forecast as
  the last word, a recap ("so, to sum up"), or a moral.
- Anything unresolved is said inside the piece ("the vote is set for
  Thursday"), not saved as a cliffhanger.

### Story shapes

Every episode takes one of these shapes, or none. The brief chooses one before anything is searched; once the packets are read, the editor may choose another. A shape is what the story usually looks like when the material supports it, never a set of boxes: **a part with nothing behind it is dropped, never filled.** Each shape below is read by the writer word for word.

**The news** (`breaking`)

- *Chosen:* Something happened and they want to know what (outside sports and business figures).
- *The shape:* open on the event itself - who did what, when - in particulars; then what led to it, because that is why it happened; but then what is disputed or unsettled in the world, said as the world's open question and never as something that was not found; therefore what it has already changed; land on the most concrete consequence that already exists - a number, a decision, a person's move; never end on what happens next, a forecast or a "we'll see".
- *Example:* None yet.

**The number** (`business`)

- *Chosen:* Results, a market move, an economic figure.
- *The shape:* open on the figure against what was expected; but then the gap, and what is surprising in it; because of the explanation, attributed to whoever gave it; therefore how the market or the people affected took it; land on the concrete figure that shows the effect - a price move, a rate, a count; never advice, "investors should", or a projection as the last line.
- *Example:* Closest: "what does it mean when the fed raises rates". A results story is still needed.

**The result** (`sports_recap`)

- *Chosen:* A game or race, when the reporting says it is final.
- *The shape:* open on the result and the reason it happened, in one or two sentences; then what was at stake; but the turn the game hinged on; because of who or what decided it; therefore what it changes - the table, the standings, a run - as fact; land on the most concrete name or stat, the player or the margin; never a preview told as the result.
- *Example:* "how did max verstappen do in the singapore sprint and what did mclaren's collision change".

**What's at stake** (`sports_preview`)

- *Chosen:* A game or event that has not happened yet.
- *The shape:* open on what is at stake and when it happens; then where each side stands, by its record; but the matchup that decides it; beside it, never as an outcome, what the forecast markets expect; land on the one fact the game turns on, or when and where it is; never a prediction stated as fact.
- *Example:* None yet.

**State of play** (`in_progress`)

- *Chosen:* Never chosen before the search: only once the packets show that something asked about has not finished.
- *The shape:* open on how it stands right now, with its time; then what is at stake; because of what has already been settled, how it stands; but what is still open - and it ends there, because it has not finished; land on the latest concrete fact, with when it was true; never an outcome, or a guess at one.
- *Example:* None yet.

**How it works** (`explainer`)

- *Chosen:* How or why something works, or why it matters.
- *The shape:* open on the one idea that makes it surprising or simple, concretely; then, because of it, the mechanism underneath; one real case of it working; therefore what follows from it; land on the concrete consequence the mechanism leads to; never a summary, a recap of the mechanism or "so next time you...".
- *Example:* "how do passkeys work", "why is the sky blue", "how do noise cancelling headphones work", "autoimmune", "what does it mean when the fed raises rates", "why does the suez canal matter so much", "why do flights get cancelled for weather that isn't where I am".

**The story from history** (`historical`)

- *Chosen:* Something in the past, told for its own sake.
- *The shape:* open on the moment it turned - a date, a place, a person; then the setup, briefly; but the catalyst; therefore the turn; therefore the consequence; land on the image or fact that closes it; never "to this day" filler, or history told as how things stand now.
- *Example:* "how did apollo 13 get home", "where did house music originate in america", "what caused the 2008 financial crisis", "why is a marathon 26.2 miles".

**Why now** (`trend`)

- *Chosen:* Something everyone is suddenly talking about.
- *The shape:* open on the specific thing that set it off, and when; then how big it actually is, as a fact - numbers, who; but the competing explanations, each attributed; therefore which part genuinely matters; land on that part, concretely; never attention claimed with no number behind it.
- *Example:* None yet.

**Who they are** (`profile`)

- *Chosen:* A question about a person.
- *The shape:* open on why they are being talked about now - what they just did; then who they are, situated; because of the two or three turns that got them here; therefore where they stand now, from dated reporting; land on the most concrete current fact about them; never a biography from memory where it can change.
- *Example:* None yet.

**One against the other** (`comparison`)

- *Chosen:* One thing set against another.
- *The shape:* open on the difference that decides it; then each side in its own terms; but where they actually differ; because of why the difference exists; land on the concrete fact that separates them; never which one the listener should choose.
- *Example:* None yet.

**No shape** (`general`): when none fits, the house voice carries it. A town's news and the weather keep the order FAM composes for them in code.

## Part 4 - Each category, the way its best reporters tell it

Each section names the outlets whose reporting is the model - for how they
tell stories, never as text to copy.

### Sports

*Models: The Athletic, ESPN, Sports Illustrated, the Associated Press
sports desk (chosen by the owner, 2026-10-29).*

- Lead with the **result and the moment that decided it** - the play, the
  goal, the drive - not the final score alone.
- Statistics in context: "his third straight 30-point game" says more than
  "34 points".
- **Results come from the scoreboard provider**, never from a preview or a
  prediction. Before a game: the stakes, the matchup, the injuries reported,
  the odds as odds.
- Say when: "Sunday night in Philadelphia".
- In-game moments tell the story; a score at halftime is not the final.

### Money and business

*Models: Bloomberg, The Wall Street Journal, CNBC, Reuters (chosen by the
owner, 2026-10-29).*

- **What moved, by how much, from what, and the reason reporters gave.** A
  move without a reported reason is said without one - never invent "on
  fears of".
- Every price has its moment ("at Friday's close") and comes from the price
  provider.
- Explain why it matters to an ordinary person in one sentence, without
  advice.
- Company claims are attributed ("the company said its sales rose").

### Tech

*Models: The Verge, TechCrunch, Wired, Ars Technica (chosen by the owner,
2026-10-29).*

- **What it does for a person**, before how it works or its specifications.
- Separate what was announced from what has shipped and what reviewers
  found.
- Name the company and the product precisely; launch dates are dates, not
  "soon".

### Science

*Models: Quanta Magazine, Scientific American, New Scientist, Nature's news
team (chosen by the owner, 2026-10-29).*

- **What was found, how it was found, and how sure the researchers are** -
  sample size, whether it is peer-reviewed or a preprint, whether it was in
  animals or people.
- One study is one study: say so, and say what it does not show.
- Explain the one idea a listener needs to follow it, simply.

### Health

*Models: STAT, KFF Health News, The New York Times Well, MedPage Today
(chosen by the owner, 2026-10-29).*

- **Facts, never advice.** What was found or decided, by whom, and for
  whom.
- Absolute risk before relative risk: "from 2 in 1,000 to 3 in 1,000", not
  only "50 percent higher".
- Approval, recall and guidance decisions are attributed to the body that
  made them.

### World news

*Models: Reuters, the Associated Press, BBC World Service, Al Jazeera English
(chosen by the owner, 2026-10-29).*

- **Who, where, and what changed today** - then the context a listener needs
  to understand it.
- Casualty and damage figures are attributed and dated ("local officials
  said on Tuesday"), and revisions are told as revisions.
- Disputed claims are attributed to each side; places are named precisely.

### Culture

*Models: The Ringer, Variety, Rolling Stone, NPR's arts and culture desk
(chosen by the owner, 2026-10-29).*

- **What it is, and why people care about it now** - the release, the
  record, the controversy, the number.
- Reviews are opinion: screened out unless the listener asks what critics
  think, and then told as the critics' view.
- Box office, charts and streams have a source and a date.

### Local news

*Models: The Philadelphia Inquirer, The Texas Tribune, Patch, Axios Local
(chosen by the owner, 2026-10-29) - reporting that names the place, the
people and the decision.*

- Name the town, the body (council, school board) and the decision.
- When a town has little reporting, say what is known plainly; never fill
  with a region's news as if it were the town's.

## Part 5 - How Shakespeare uses this

- The outline names, for each beat, the packet sentences that carry it (by
  number), and which part of this playbook the beat follows: the opening
  (Part 3), the category's way of telling it (Part 4), the ear (Part 2).
- The writer receives the outline and this playbook; Mclovin checks the
  facts against the packets as each sentence is written.
- **Measured without hand-scoring** (the owner, 2026-10-28: "we would rather
  not have it be so hands on to quality check episodes"): the quality
  checker's code measures (§309: opening, flow, depth, repetition, hook,
  ending), Mclovin's verdicts, and listening (how many leave in the first
  twenty seconds; how many hear 85%). A version of this playbook stays if
  those improve.
